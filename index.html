Welcome to Paper Trader

This is a "paper trading" account — the real term investors use for practicing with pretend money before (or instead of) risking real cash. You started with $10,000 in play money and a list of real companies. Their prices are the actual closing prices from the stock market — the same numbers a real investor sees, just refreshed once a day instead of every second.

Head to the Watchlist tab to get started — a Beginner Path banner will walk you through seven small milestones, one tip at a time, with a suggested starter size along the way. Check your progress anytime below, or turn the tips off if you don't need them.

This app is laid out the way a real teen brokerage account (like Schwab's Teen Investor account) tends to be — an account-value card up top, a Watchlist, Positions, and History, and a "market order" trade ticket — so the layout and vocabulary feel familiar if you move to a real account later. It isn't affiliated with or built by any brokerage; it's just modeled on that style.

Training wheels

Your Beginner Path

Seven small milestones that cover everything worth knowing before you're on your own — not just your first trade, but selling, diversifying, and trying something riskier too. Do them in any order; the Watchlist tab always points you at whichever one is next.

How to play

Four steps to your first trade

1

Browse the Watchlist tab

Stocks are grouped into sectors — Tech & Innovation, Entertainment & Gaming, Everyday Brands, and Rising Stars. Tap any company to open its card and see its price and recent trend.

2

Pick Buy or Sell, and how many shares

Use the − / + buttons or type a number. The card shows exactly what it'll cost and how much cash you'll have left before you confirm anything.

3

Start small and spread it out

You don't have to spend all $10,000 on day one. Try putting a little into two or three different sectors instead of all of it into one stock — that's diversification, and it's rule one for a reason.

4

Track it in Positions and History

Positions shows what each holding is worth right now and whether it's up or down since you bought it. History is your full trade record — a record you can look back on to see what you'd do differently.

How often should I check in?

Prices here update once a day, after the real market closes — so checking every hour won't show anything new, and that's on purpose. A good rhythm: look in once a day, or even just two or three times a week.

Each time, ask yourself: what moved the most today, and can I guess why? That habit — noticing, then wondering why — is most of what investing actually is.

What to watch for

Today's change

The green or red number on each stock, and the pill at the top. Tap "Why up/down today?" under your account value for a quick read on whether it looks like a market-wide move or something about one company.

All-time performance

The number at the top of the app. This is the one that actually matters — how you're doing since you started, not today.

Your allocation

The bar in the Positions tab. If one color takes over the whole bar, you've gone all-in on one bet — worth noticing.

Patterns over weeks

One bad day means little. A stock drifting the same direction for two or three weeks straight is more worth a closer look.

The basics

What is a share of stock?

When you buy a share, you're buying a tiny sliver of ownership in a real company. If Apple does well — sells lots of iPhones, makes more profit — more people want to own a piece of it, and the price tends to rise. If a company struggles, the price tends to fall.

What's a ticker symbol?

The short code in capital letters next to each company — like AAPL for Apple or KO for Coca-Cola. It's just shorthand real investors use so they don't have to type the full company name every time.

Why do prices go up and down every day?

Prices move on news, earnings reports, what people expect the company to do next, and just general buying and selling. Day-to-day wiggles are normal and mostly noise — they don't always mean something important happened.

What's diversification, and why bother?

Diversification means spreading your money across different companies and sectors instead of betting it all on one. If one stock has a bad week, the others can balance it out. It won't stop you from ever losing money, but it smooths out the ride.

Should I panic if a stock drops?

Real investors ask "has anything actually changed about this company," not just "did the price move." A lot of the biggest investing mistakes come from selling in a panic right after a normal dip, then missing the recovery.

What's the difference between price and value?

Price is just what the market currently agrees a share is worth — it can be moved by mood and headlines in the short term. Value is what a company is actually worth based on how well it's really doing. The two usually converge over the long run, but not always instantly.

Avg. cost, market value, gain/loss — what do those mean?

Avg. cost is the average price you paid per share across all your purchases. Market value is what your shares are worth right now at the current price. Gain/loss is the difference between the two — it's only "real" money once you actually sell.

Going deeper

Level up: how the market really works

What's a bull market vs. a bear market?

A bull market is a stretch where prices are broadly rising and people feel optimistic. A bear market is the opposite — a sustained slide, usually defined as a drop of 20% or more from a recent high. Both are normal parts of a cycle that repeats over decades; no bull or bear market has ever lasted forever.

What's "market cap," and why does company size matter?

Market capitalization is a company's total value — share price multiplied by the number of shares that exist. A large-cap company (like the ones in Tech & Innovation here) tends to be established and steadier. A small-cap company (like most of Rising Stars & Growth Picks) has more room to grow fast, but also more room to fall — less of a track record backing up the price.

What's "short selling," and can I do it here?

Every trade in this app is a normal purchase: you buy low hoping to sell high later. Short selling is the reverse bet — an investor borrows shares they don't own, sells them immediately, and hopes to buy them back later at a lower price to return to the lender, pocketing the difference. It's a real strategy professionals use, but it flips the usual risk upside down: when you buy a stock, the most you can lose is what you paid, but when you short one, there's no ceiling on how high a price can climb, so the potential loss is technically unlimited. This game doesn't offer shorting, on purpose — it's an advanced, higher-risk move that's easy to misjudge, and mastering ordinary buying and selling first is worth far more to a beginner. Think of it as a term worth recognizing, not a tool you're missing.

What's a dividend?

Some companies pay shareholders a small, regular cash amount just for holding their stock — a slice of the company's profits, usually sent out every quarter. Coca-Cola and McDonald's are classic dividend payers; fast-growing companies like the ones in Rising Stars usually pay none, preferring to reinvest profits into growing faster instead.

What's an IPO?

An initial public offering is the day a company first sells shares to the public and starts trading on a stock exchange — before that, it's privately owned. Reddit (2024) and Cava (2023) are both real, fairly recent IPOs among the stocks in this game. A freshly public company's stock often swings more than an established one, simply because there's less history to judge it by yet.

What is "volatility"?

Volatility is how much a stock's price moves around, in either direction, over time. A steady blue-chip like Coca-Cola might barely move 1–2% in a normal week. A Rising Stars pick can easily swing 5–10% on a single piece of news. Higher volatility isn't automatically bad — it just means bigger possible gains and bigger possible losses, so it deserves a smaller slice of a beginner's money.

What's a "market order" vs. a "limit order"?

A market order buys or sells immediately at whatever the current price is — that's what every trade in this game does. A limit order instead says "only buy if the price drops to $X" (or "only sell at $Y or higher"), and waits until the market reaches that price, if it ever does. Real trading apps offer both; this one keeps things simple with market orders only.

What's an index fund, and how is it different from picking stocks yourself?

An index fund is a single investment that holds a small slice of hundreds or thousands of companies at once, tracking a market index like the S&P 500. Instead of betting on which individual companies will do well — which is what you're practicing here — it spreads the bet across the whole market. Most professional investors can't reliably beat index funds over the long run, which is why they're the backbone of most real long-term portfolios, even though picking stocks yourself (like in this game) is a great way to learn how the market actually behaves.
